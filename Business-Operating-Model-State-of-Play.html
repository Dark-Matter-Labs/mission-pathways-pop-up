In five minutes

What's actually driving the cost and complexity of how Dm is built

If you read nothing else on this page, read this section — everything below just backs it up.

Dm operates as a group of legal entities — the UK, a Dutch BV, Sweden, South Korea, and (until this year) Canada — built up one contract, one government requirement, one tax jurisdiction at a time. No single decision produced this shape; it accreted. The result is a structure nobody currently holds as living knowledge: basic questions about why it's set up this way could not be answered from memory in more than one working session.Said

That matters because the shape of the group is not free. Multiple entities sharing staff across borders triggers transfer pricing, which requires time recording, which — on top of being a genuine cost-recognition necessity for grant-funded work — is also actively corroding collaboration inside the organisation. Underneath that sits a second, quieter problem: some of the data this needs (unfunded time, fee income by funding type) already exists but isn't yet embedded into standing reporting, and benchmarking central capacity against other organisations is genuinely difficult, because the cross-entity staff-sharing model itself is unusual.Inference

The single sentence version

Dm's operating model wasn't designed for the shape it's now in — entities, staff and time-recording rules that once solved specific contract or compliance problems now generate an ongoing tax, admin and cultural cost that nobody has stepped back to re-decide, and the organisation doesn't yet hold the data it would need to make that re-decision well.Inference

Why it's built this way

Five entities, five separate reasons — none of them wrong on their own

Each entity was a rational answer to a specific problem at the time. The complexity is emergent, not designed.

EntityStatusWhy it exists
United Kingdom Active · strongest Most diversified entity — higher day-rate work alongside cost-reporting contracts, and has attracted the most philanthropic funding, including core funding (which is itself hard to distribute usefully across the rest of the group).Said
Netherlands (BV) Active · largest contract Set up to hold Dm's largest single contract, a European Commission-funded programme, which needed a European entity to run through. Dutch law has no not-for-profit company status as such; the choice was a standard BV with an asset lock in its articles, rather than a foundation — though it's since been confirmed a foundation wouldn't have avoided the VAT exposure described below either; that liability doesn't turn on which structure was chosen. Nobody could recall how the original choice was made.Said
Sweden Active · loss-making Needed to hold government-agency contracts locally, and heavily dependent on that cost-reporting income. Has hit a state-aid "de-minimis" threshold that treats its work as economic activity; above that threshold, further public funding requires roughly 40% co-funding. A fix is in progress: forming an association with an external partner to have the work reclassified as non-economic. A second, compounding cause of the losses sits underneath that — see below.Said
South Korea Active · loss-making, low visibility Set up around a specific government contract, on a similar cost-reporting basis. The group has materially less financial visibility into it than the other entities and relies on locally produced accounts; whether its current activity is sustainable hasn't yet been independently assessed.Said
Canada Closed, 2026 Dissolved deliberately as a complexity-reduction step, and named in the session as a clean example of that working.Said

Sweden: two compounding causes, not one

The de-minimis threshold isn't the whole story. The transfer-pricing rate charged for staff working on Swedish contracts hasn't been fully covering the actual cost of that time — Swedish employer social security contributions run at 31.2% of gross salary, well above the group's other jurisdictions, and until recently the group used one averaged day rate across all staff rather than each person's actual rate. That's being corrected now, with individual day rates feeding the transfer-pricing calculation, which should help independently of the de-minimis fix. Viability isn't riding on that fix alone either: other funding sources are being actively explored, though nothing identified yet would cover the entity's costs on its own over the next year.Said

"The reason we're only realising now we've got a permanent establishment in Spain is because the team has grown." On how growth quietly creates new compliance exposure the group didn't design for — the same dynamic is now being checked for Germany.Said

Spain: leaning toward accepting the exposure, not arguing it away

Spanish tax authorities are understood to be significantly more punitive than the UK's, and arguments against permanent-establishment status there are expected to carry little weight — so the working assumption has shifted from "avoid it" toward "plan for it": setting up a formal local presence deliberately, on a timeline, rather than waiting to be caught out. Maintaining a permanent team there through the Oyster arrangement is itself part of what's created this exposure, understood now as a real risk of sanctions rather than just a compliance technicality — with the same question being assessed for Germany. That raises an immediate, unresolved question of its own: whoever becomes the entity's legal representative in Spain needs to sit outside Spain and hold no assets there, to limit personal exposure to enforcement — and that person hasn't yet been decided.Said

The thread underneath all five is the same: people employed by one entity routinely work on another entity's funded contracts. That is the single biggest lever behind the group's cost base — it is what creates the transfer-pricing obligation, and transfer pricing is what makes time recording a legal necessity rather than a management preference, on top of its separate use in grant cost-reporting.Inference

What the structure costs us

Four cost drivers, all downstream of the same root cause

None of these are mismanagement in isolation. They are the predictable price of running a small organisation like a multinational.

VAT — roughly £100k a year, unrecoverable
Netherlands (BV)

Grant income is VAT-exempt, but a large share of the BV's real costs — professional fees, and the cross-entity staff time charged in via transfer pricing — fall outside that exemption. Net Zero Cities' own rules only allow VAT to be recovered on freelance costs directly attributable to the project itself; VAT on transfer-pricing charges (staff or freelancer costs seconded in from other entities) and VAT on professional fees — both significant — fall outside what the grant can recover. That's the actual mechanism behind the ~£100k/yr figure, not an unclaimed opportunity: the BV already claims what the rules allow. Roughly £30k has already gone into professional advice investigating a further fix, without one yet found; simply raising the transfer-pricing margin to cover it was floated and ruled out, since it would just mean paying more VAT rather than saving any.Said

Professional fees and the Oyster (EOR) contract

£50k has gone on professional fees year-to-date, now under active review with those firms asked to justify the spend. Separately, Dm uses an Employer-of-Record service (Oyster) to employ staff in countries without a local entity — adopted to cut the internal admin burden of employing people directly worldwide, but its fees are now judged "extortionate," with termination planned by year end in favour of local tax and employment advisers doing the same compliance work more cheaply.Said

The year-end date isn't arbitrary: Oyster's annual fee — roughly £30k — isn't prorated, so missing the December deadline means paying it again in full for another year with nothing to show for it. That fee is what's forcing the pace, since the unwind itself depends on outside timelines (someone has to physically register with the relevant tax and social security authorities in each country) that Dm doesn't fully control.Said

The same growth that made Oyster necessary has now produced a side-effect Oyster was meant to avoid: a director based in Spain creates indicators of a taxable "permanent establishment" there, and maintaining a permanent team through Oyster has itself become part of that exposure — understood now as a real risk of sanctions, not just a compliance technicality, with the same question being assessed for Germany too. Unwinding people from Oyster onto direct local employment is itself costly, which is part of why the change is being timed to the contract renewal rather than done immediately.Said

Time recording and cost reporting

EU and government cost-reporting contracts require time recording for two separate reasons: to recognise revenue on progress-based (not deliverables-based) contracts, and to produce a legally defensible number for what one entity owes another for shared staff time. An estimated ≥£60k gap exists in Net Zero Cities time recording this year alone — though follow-up conversation suggests part of that is people not doing the funded work forecast for their role, a resourcing and forecasting issue as much as a compliance one, not purely under-logging.Said

The one part of the org that has already stepped off this model

One team is already working on a deliverables basis, outside time recording entirely — but only because it separately built its own financial buffer to be able to afford working that way. It's a live proof that another model is possible, not yet a model the rest of the organisation can adopt without the same buffer.Said

People, time and capacity

The behavioural cost is as real as the financial one

And much of the data that would show its shape clearly exists already — it just isn't yet built into how the organisation reports on itself.

Time recording's cultural cost

Named directly in the session, not inferred: time recording drives hoarding of work, protection of work, isolation, and lower collaboration across the organisation. It's treated as a necessity under current financial scarcity and cost-reporting contracts, not as something anyone believes works well long-term.Said

Unfunded drift, with no trigger to stop it

People hired against one funded contract can drift into unfunded work elsewhere with no automatic checkpoint — becoming an "invisible cost" carried for months before anyone notices. One anonymised example from the session: an employee funded against Net Zero Cities time has been generating only small, ad hoc income (roughly £5k at a time) from unrelated work, on a permanent contract that would be expensive to unwind.Said

Central capacity is thin, and nobody knows if that's a problem

Finance runs at 2.7 FTE against a group of roughly 50–55 FTE across five jurisdictions. This isn't one person's read of the situation — it's been raised independently, more than once, in different rooms with different people in them: the organisation carries too much structural complexity for its size, and needs a materially bigger operational team to hold it properly. Benchmarking that against other organisations is difficult in a specific way, not just from a lack of survey data: the cross-entity staff-sharing model itself is unusual, and that model is a meaningful source of the complexity finance has to manage, not just the headcount it has to cover — so even a same-size comparator elsewhere may not be carrying the same load.Said

The thinness compounds because so much of this work resists delegation: it depends on background and context — knowing the tax history, holding the relationships with local advisers, remembering why a decision was made — that sits in one or two people's heads rather than being written down anywhere. Spare capacity elsewhere in the organisation genuinely exists, but handing this specific work over isn't simply a matter of finding free hours; the knowledge itself would need to move first, and nothing currently makes that happen.Said

Underneath both problems sits a reporting gap rather than a data gap. Unfunded time and fee income by funding type are both already tracked at entity and project level — they just haven't been embedded into standing monthly reporting, a resourcing casualty of being stretched rather than neglect. One refinement is still needed even once that's done: grants are currently grouped together without distinguishing a government contract from a philanthropic gift, which needs separating out to model income by funding type properly. Both are now moving, with a recent addition to the finance function being lined up to help take this from ad hoc into standing practice.Said

Open structural questions

Raised directly, and explicitly left open

None of these were decided. They're recorded here so the next round starts from where this one stopped, rather than relitigating the same ground.

  1. Should entities be legally separated from the group, rather than run as one group with cross-entity staffing? Raised directly, and the practical counter-argument holds up under scrutiny: as long as staff keep being shared across entities, they'd still need to invoice each other whether legally separated or not — so separation on its own doesn't remove the underlying complexity. Whether it would still be worth it for other reasons (ring-fencing liability, enabling a different business model in one entity) hasn't been tested against that reality yet.Said
  2. Should deliverables-based contracting become the default, rather than the exception? It removes the need for time recording and its behavioural cost, but requires a financial buffer most teams don't have, and most current income (grant and government) isn't shaped that way to begin with.Inference
  3. Should there be an explicit portfolio-balance decision, instead of "if it's aligned to our objectives, we do it"? Both people in the session agreed there's currently no shared, revisited view of which mission areas the organisation is actively resourcing versus simply accepting work in — and separately agreed that a functional, operational entity ecosystem is itself a precondition for the mission, not a distraction from it.Said
  4. Where is the organisation holding optionality open that it should instead decide on? Sweden was named as the live example: no current mission-pathways work there, and its viability under active review on three fronts at once — the de-minimis fix, the transfer-pricing correction, and a search for other funding sources that hasn't yet found enough to cover the entity's costs on its own.Said
  5. Should legal directorship be distributed across more people, rather than concentrated in one? One individual currently holds legal directorship on most of Dm's entities — carrying a large share of the group's personal legal risk alone, which runs directly against the organisation's own stated goal of decentralised risk and shared accountability. Work is underway to understand what level of risk any given directorship actually carries before deciding who else should hold it, but the concentration itself is acknowledged and not yet resolved.Said

Where we can evolve the model

Candidate directions — not decisions

Some of these already have a clear owner among the seventeen experiments above. Two of the biggest levers don't yet, and are flagged as such.

  1. Decide the entity question on purpose, on a timebox. Not "should we separate entities" as an open-ended debate — simple legal separation looks weak on its own, since shared staffing would still require cross-entity invoicing either way — but a scoped decision about which geographies justify a standing legal entity at all, given the compliance cost each one carries. A working group is now being convened to look at entity structure; worth making sure it's explicitly tied to EXP #16 rather than running in parallel to it.
  2. Build financial abundance as a deliberate, group-level capability — not something one team has to invent for itself — so more of the organisation can step off time recording where the funding shape allows it. No clear current owner among the seventeen — the closest, EXP #10–12, are about acquiring capital rather than about how internal buffers get built and allocated. This is arguably the biggest single gap in the present roadmap.
  3. Make the funding-mix picture (income by type, by entity) standing infrastructure, not a one-off pulled together under urgency. Nearest owner: EXP #17 — worth naming this specific dataset as an early deliverable rather than leaving it implicit in "operational information flows."
  4. Set a benchmark for "how thin is too thin" for central capacity (finance, and likely ops more broadly) relative to group size and entity complexity. No current owner — a natural addition under EXP #8 or EXP #17.
  5. Connect the trigger points that already exist in principle. EXP #4, #5 and #15 each independently point toward catching unfunded drift — the evolution is making sure they meet at one shared checkpoint (when someone's funded time runs out) instead of three separate practices that each cover part of the problem.
  6. Look for one generalisable pattern for "local presence" exposure, rather than solving Sweden's de-minimis threshold and Germany's permanent-establishment question separately, each time growth creates a new one. Nearest owner: EXP #16.

How other organisations make cost-reporting projects work — checked against Dm's own figuresSaid
External research, first drafted 21 Sep 2026, since checked directly against the BV's actual Net Zero Cities claims.

Both levers below have since been checked against reality, rather than left as things worth trying. The 25% flat-rate indirect-cost allowance — which every Horizon Europe beneficiary can claim against eligible direct costs, no supporting documentation required — is already being claimed in full. Non-deductible VAT as an eligible cost is also already in use, but only within its real limits: Net Zero Cities' rules let it be recovered on freelance costs directly attributable to the project, not on VAT arising from transfer-pricing charges or professional fees — both of which are significant. That's the actual explanation for the ~£100k/yr unrecoverable-VAT figure above: it isn't an unclaimed opportunity, it's the genuine edge of what the grant's own rules allow to be recovered.

Time recording also isn't universal across Horizon Europe — it depends on which cost model a given contract uses. Actual-cost grants require timesheets; lump-sum grants (paid per work package or deliverable) don't require timesheets to be submitted at all, only internal records showing the work genuinely happened; and a middle option, "personnel unit costs," lets a beneficiary use one calculated daily rate from its annual accounts instead of tracking each person's actual time individually. Switching isn't simple in practice, though: moving to an alternative cost model requires a lengthy approval process at the start of each Net Zero Cities contract, and it isn't clear the alternative would cover Dm's actual costs as well as the current model does — worth exploring further if the resource exists to do it properly, but not an easy win to bank on.

One important wrinkle, still unconfirmed: Net Zero Cities is coordinated by EIT Climate-KIC as a 33-partner, 13-country consortium, and runs a two-tier structure — a core consortium funded directly via a Framework Partnership Agreement with the European Commission, and other participants brought in as sub-grantees under a separate Award Agreement with Climate-KIC. The general Horizon Europe rules above apply cleanly to the core consortium's EC grant agreement; a sub-grant agreement with Climate-KIC could carry different, stricter terms. Which of these Dm actually sits under is worth confirming directly rather than assumed.Unverified
